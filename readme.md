Unauthorized Drone Combat in Low-Altitude Economy: Joint Communications and Interference Based on MIMO Cellular System  
Abstract: To ensure the vigorous development of the low-altitude economy, combating unauthorized drones (UAVs) is a critical task. The widespread deployment of base stations has great potential for joint communications and interference. In view of this, this paper studies the design of beamforming for joint communication and interference based on a dual-function multi-input multi-output (MIMO) cellular system, aiming to support communication with legitimate users while counteracting unauthorized drones simultaneously. Firstly, the joint communication and interference (JCJ) problem is constructed, and it is relaxed through semidefinite relaxation (SDR) method, resulting in an easily solvable semidefinite programming (SDP) problem - SDR provides a crucial step for simplifying complex JCJ designs. Although the solution to the relaxed SDP problem cannot be directly solved for the original problem, it can provide important insights for subsequent optimization. Therefore, this paper designs a new type of constraint for the structure of the SDP problem to ensure that the solution satisfies the rank-1 constraint of the original problem. Finally, extensive simulations validate the effectiveness of the proposed JCJ scheme, showing that when the total number of legitimate users and unauthorized drones exceeds the number of antennas, the proposed JCJ scheme still works effectively.  
Introduction: The Low-Altitude Economy (LAE) is an economic system that integrates various low-altitude aviation activities, involving drones and electric vertical takeoff and landing (eVTOL) aircraft among other unmanned and manned aviation vehicles [1]. The LAE holds significant promise in providing abundant low-altitude services in transportation, environmental monitoring, agriculture, entertainment, and other fields, offering both economic and social benefits, which have sparked widespread research interest [2]-[6].
To achieve the successful development of low-altitude economy, it is not only necessary to ensure continuous wireless communication with authorized drones but also to implement necessary countermeasures against unauthorized drones [7], [8]. Typically, countermeasure technologies can be divided into two main categories: physical capture and jamming. Physical capture methods are efficient and cost-effective but may not be user-friendly for operators. Jamming is the most commonly used method to neutralize drones entering restricted areas, which can be categorized into four types: the first type involves transmitting noise non-structured signals that reduce the Signal-to-Interference-plus-Noise Ratio (SINR) of unauthorized drones, as in the literature [10] where partial band noise interference was used to cut off the control link between the drone and the controller; the second type involves broadcasting pseudo-GPS signals that guide unauthorized drones out of the restricted area; the third type involves cracking the communication protocol of unauthorized drones to generate false commands to steer them away from the restricted area; the fourth type involves passive jamming, which utilizes passive devices to modify the signals in the environment, causing multipath signals to cancel each other at the receiver end, a common method being the design of reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RIS) phase [15]-[18].
Traditional counter-drone methods require specialized equipment, platforms, and architectures [8], significantly increasing the cost of combating unauthorized drones. However, the potential of widely deployed multiple input multiple output (MIMO) base stations (BS) for this purpose has not been fully exploited. As dense cellular infrastructure evolves towards integrated perception and communication (ISAC) capabilities, utilizing base stations to counter unauthorized drones becomes a highly promising direction. In addition to providing communication and perception services, base stations can also implement effective countermeasures to ensure the lawful and orderly use of airspace while reducing the need for expensive dedicated equipment.
The existing ISAC (In-Situ Synthetic Aperture Radar) research focuses on utilizing a unified hardware platform and/or waveforms to simultaneously achieve communication data demodulation and target parameter extraction in noisy observation scenarios [20]-[22]. For example, the literature [23] jointly senses user activity, channel, and position in ultra-large MIMO systems; the paper [24] proposes an ISAC processing method based on compression sampling; the paper [25] acquires the uplink channel and position of user equipment (UE) in terahertz ultra-large array systems; the paper [26] introduces an ISAC system based on perception center index modulation. Given the potential applications of UAVs across various industries, there has been a significant amount of research combining UAVs with ISAC [1], [27], [28].
However, the above ISAC studies have not considered countermeasures against unauthorized user devices. The literature [29] extensively reviews various interference and anti-interference methods. To address the interference challenges during communication, researchers have developed dedicated waveforms to maximize detection capabilities [30]; furthermore, methods have been explored that leverage interference signal echoes for perception while preserving communication functions, allowing dynamic resource allocation based on perception results [31]. The literature [32] employs deep reinforcement learning (DRL) methods to interfere with frequency hopping spread spectrum devices using dedicated interference devices; the literature [33] investigates full-duplex systems involving single user devices and single UAVs, detecting and interfering with unauthorized drones through the uplink signals of the user device; the literature [34] thoroughly studies the simultaneous communication and interference issues with channel state information (CSI) errors; the literature [35] proposes a novel method of generating electromagnetic signals using optical devices, achieving secure communication while performing radar distance and speed deception interference; empirical tests indicate that low-energy narrowband signals can effectively interfere with fifth-generation networks [36].
The literature [37] proposed the concept of integrating radar, communication, and jamming for the purpose of disrupting unauthorized user equipment. However, its research premises revolve around security, assuming that the target of interference is a eavesdropper, which has limitations and does not apply to all unauthorized drone flight scenarios (unauthorized drones may not involve eavesdropping behavior). Therefore, further research is needed to address the challenge of interfering with unauthorized general unmanned aerial vehicles. In recent years, researchers have proposed the Integrated Communication and Jamming (ICAJ) framework, which enhances communication and jamming within the same system to achieve more reliable communication and efficient jamming [38],[39]. However, the above studies assume that communication and jamming data streams are separate, whereas in reality, it is possible to achieve more coordinated ICAJ by jointly designing both communication and jamming data streams.
In addition to active interference techniques, passive methods have also received attention. When RIS acts as a collaborator, it can effectively cover blind spots and improve signal quality; when RIS acts as an interferer, it can reconfigure the multipath signals through its channel, causing phase-cancellation interference at the receiver [15]-[17]. Meanwhile, anti-interference research has also made significant progress. Literature [40] studied the dynamic interaction between the jammer and the base station, where the base station adjusts the beamformer based on the jammer's interference power; literature [41] focused on designing robust passive beamforming using RIS to combat interference even if the angle information is inaccurate; literature [42] proposed a method of remodulating the interference signal using additional transceivers in the environment, embedding structured information for potential use. The continuous advancement of interference and anti-interference technologies mutually promotes the continuous development of both fields.
Essentially, the integration of ISAC with drone combat relies on downlink beamforming as one of the key technologies. There are numerous classic solutions: in terms of full-digital beamforming, there are well-known schemes based on channel inversion (CI) [43], linear minimum mean square error (LMMSE) [44], and optimization-based schemes [45]-[47]; in terms of hybrid beamforming, there are schemes based on semidefinite programming (SDP) [48], orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP) [49], and alternating minimization [50]. Thanks to the development of deep learning, some advanced data-driven beamforming schemes have also emerged [51]-[53]. However, according to our knowledge, there is currently no universal downlink beamforming solution for integrating communication with unauthorized drone combat scenarios.
The contributions of this paper are as follows:
1  
A joint communication and interference (JCJ) model was constructed and related optimization problems were formulated. This model demonstrates how base stations can efficiently counter unauthorized drones while meeting basic communication requirements with minimal power consumption. Through theoretical analysis of the constructed problem, this paper proposes that effective countermeasures against unauthorized drones can be seamlessly integrated into user equipment (UEs) data streams without the need for additional interference flows.
2  
After a thorough analysis of the structure of the proposed JCJ optimization problem, an additional constraint was designed: in complex symmetric matrices, the sum of real parts of any diagonal elements parallel to the main diagonal (including cyclic diagonals) should not be less than a fraction of the matrix trace (between 0 and 1). This constraint ensures that the solution obtained through SDP meets the rank-1 constraint, and simulations validate the effectiveness of this constraint.
3  
Since the proposed JCJ scheme does not require additional signal flows to interfere with unauthorized drones, it can still work effectively when the total number of user equipment (UEs) exceeds the number of transmitting antennas. Moreover, when the number of transmitting antennas is less than 32, the proposed JCJ scheme consistently outperforms traditional CI schemes.
Method Introduction The proposed Joint Communications and Interference (JCJ) scheme mainly includes four core parts: problem transformation, problem reconfiguration, algorithm design, and complexity and convergence analysis.
1. The problem of converting the original JCJ (P1) constraints into quadratic constraints is difficult to solve directly. It is first equivalently transformed into a semidefinite programming (SDP) problem (P2), where new matrix variables are defined and the original problem's rate constraint and SINR constraint are converted into matrix trace constraints. Since P2 contains rank-1 constraints (, solving it directly remains challenging. Therefore, by removing the rank-1 constraints through semidefinite relaxation (SDR), the relaxed SDP problem (P3) is obtained, which can be solved using convex optimization tools such as CVX. However, the solution of P3 often does not satisfy the rank-1 constraints, and when there are legitimate users, it cannot be transformed into the solution of P2 by feature value decomposition. Therefore, further optimization is required.
2. Problem Reconstruction: By analyzing the decomposition structures of P2 and P3, it is found that SDR will cause the related sub-blocks to be block diagonalized, affecting the satisfaction of rank 1 constraints. This constraint can be broken to allow diagonalization of the block, which allows it to be decomposed into an outer product of two identical vectors, thereby satisfying the rank-1 requirement. The restructured problem (P3') remains an SDP problem and can be solved using convex optimization tools.

The conclusion focuses on the ubiquitous countermeasure against unauthorized drones using widely deployed MIMO base stations, with this paper focusing on beamforming joint design to construct a dual-function system that can communicate with legitimate users and interfere with unauthorized drones simultaneously. Firstly, the JCJ problem was constructed, and it was converted into a standard SDP problem through SDR; by analyzing the problem structure, it was found that countermeasures against unauthorized drones could be achieved without the need for dedicated perturbation flows. Subsequently, an innovative constraint was proposed to ensure that the reconstructed problem solution satisfies the rank-1 constraint; finally, extensive simulations validated the correctness and effectiveness of the proposed JCJ scheme, leading to significant conclusions.
Simulation results show that the proposed JCJ scheme has significant advantages in scenarios with fewer antennas or when the total number of legitimate users and unauthorized drones exceeds the number of antennas. Specifically, when the expected SINR threshold is lower, the number of unauthorized drones is higher, and the number of transmitting antennas is smaller, the advantage of the JCJ scheme over traditional channel inversion (CI) scheme becomes more pronounced, and it can still effectively work even when the total number of devices exceeds the number of antennas (where traditional scheme fails).
However, the JCJ scheme proposed in this paper does not integrate perception functions; the joint design of communication, perception, and countermeasures is an important future research direction. Additionally, how to coordinate communication, perception, and interference in scenarios with different communication and interference bands is worth further exploration. Designing hybrid beamformers based on communication signals to interfere with unauthorized drones directly within mixed beamforming architectures is also a promising area of research. Furthermore, utilizing the beamforming characteristics of ultra-large arrays for efficient interference tasks may become a hotspot for future research.
